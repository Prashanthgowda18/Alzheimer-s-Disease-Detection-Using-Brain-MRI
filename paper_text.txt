Abstract—Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia worldwide, affecting over 55 million people. Early and accurate detection is critical, yet conventional clinical diagnosis depends on time-consuming radiological interpretation of Brain MRI scans that is expensive and prone to inter-observer variability. This paper proposes a deep learning-based system for automated multi-class classification of Alzheimer’s Disease from Brain MRI images using ResNet50 with Transfer Learning. ResNet50 is a 50-layer deep Convolutional Neural Network that employs residual (skip) connections to overcome the vanishing gradient problem in very deep networks. Pretrained on ImageNet, the final classification layer is adapted for four Alzheimer’s stages: Non-Demented, Very Mild Demented, Mild Demented, and Moderate Demented. The pipeline includes MRI preprocessing, resizing, normalization, and data augmentation before feature extraction and multi-class classification. The trained model is deployed as a web-based prediction system that outputs the predicted Alzheimer’s stage along with a confidence score. This system is intended as an AI-assisted research and screening prototype and does not replace professional medical diagnosis.
Index Terms—Alzheimer’s Disease, Deep Learning, ResNet50, Transfer Learning, Brain MRI, Convolutional Neural Network, Multi-class Classification, Image Preprocessing, Data Augmentation, Healthcare Informatics, Web Application, Confidence Score.
I. INTRODUCTION
Alzheimer’s Disease is the most prevalent cause of dementia, accounting for 60–70% of all dementia cases globally. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that over 55 million people currently live with dementia, a number projected to reach 139 million by 2050 [1]. AD is a progressive condition characterized by neuronal loss, cortical atrophy, and hippocampal shrinkage that are visible on Brain MRI scans. Despite its scale, AD remains significantly underdiagnosed in its early stages, largely because subtle structural changes in MRI are difficult to identify without specialist expertise.
Current clinical diagnosis relies on a combination of neuropsychological assessments, biomarker analysis, and manual interpretation of neuroimaging data. These methods are time-consuming, costly, and subject to inter-observer disagreement—especially for distinguishing adjacent stages such as Very Mild Demented and Non-Demented, where MRI differences are minimal. Many patients receive a definitive diagnosis only at the moderate or severe stage, by which point disease-modifying intervention is far less effective. This underscores the urgent need for automated, objective, and accessible early-stage detection tools.
Deep learning, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), has demonstrated remarkable capability in extracting hierarchical visual features from medical images. Transfer learning—reusing representations learned from large-scale datasets like ImageNet for domain-specific tasks—has proven especially effective where labelled medical imaging data is limited. Among modern CNN architectures, ResNet50 is notable for its use of residual connections, which allow gradients to propagate through 50 layers without vanishing, enabling deeper and more expressive feature extraction than traditional CNNs [2].
Most published AD classification systems address only binary detection (AD vs. Normal) and lack practical deployment infrastructure. There is a clear need for a system that: (a) performs four-class Alzheimer’s stage classification from Brain MRI; (b) uses a robust deep learning model such as ResNet50 with Transfer Learning; and (c) is accessible as a web-based application providing clinically meaningful outputs including a confidence score. This paper presents exactly such a system.
The objectives of this work are: (1) To develop a ResNet50-based Transfer Learning model for multi-class Alzheimer’s Disease classification from Brain MRI images across four stages. (2) To implement an effective MRI preprocessing and data augmentation pipeline to improve model robustness and generalization. (3) To deploy the trained model as a web-based prediction system that provides Alzheimer’s stage prediction with a confidence score.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews related work. Section III presents the proposed methodology. Section IV describes the system architecture. Section V covers implementation details. Section VI reports experimental results. Section VII outlines future scope. Section VIII concludes the paper.
II. LITERATURE SURVEY
A. CNN-Based Alzheimer’s Detection
Bansal et al. [1] proposed a multi-modal framework for Alzheimer’s and dementia detection using OASIS clinical data and MRI scans. Clinical features were optimized using a Firefly Algorithm and passed into a deep neural network, while MRI images were processed through a hybrid AlexNet-101–SVM architecture. Decision-level fusion of the two branches achieved 93.3% accuracy and a 92.1% F1-score on the test set, outperforming multiple unimodal baselines. However, the system was not deployed as a clinical tool and did not provide explainability for individual predictions.
Nandan et al. [2] surveyed machine learning approaches—including SVM, Random Forest, and deep learning—applied to early dementia detection using demographic, cognitive, and neuroimaging data. The survey identified recurring challenges: non-standardized datasets, limited performance of single-modality models, and the gap between research accuracy and practical clinical utility.
B. Transfer Learning for Brain MRI Classification
Khan et al. [3] applied VGG16 and ResNet50 with Transfer Learning on the ADNI Brain MRI dataset, incorporating data augmentation and preprocessing. Their best model achieved 97.3% accuracy for binary AD vs. Normal classification, confirming that ImageNet-pretrained features transfer effectively to neuroimaging tasks even with relatively small datasets. This validates the Transfer Learning approach adopted in the proposed system.
Liu et al. [4] designed a multi-scale CNN with an attention mechanism applied to Brain MRI volumes, reaching 95.8% accuracy through multi-scale feature fusion. Their design captures structural abnormalities at multiple resolutions, which is particularly relevant for distinguishing adjacent Alzheimer’s stages with subtle visual differences.
C. Explainability and Visualization
Rajput et al. [5] integrated Grad-CAM with a CNN-based AD classifier, achieving 94.7% accuracy while producing visual heatmaps highlighting disease-relevant brain regions such as the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex. Clinician feedback confirmed that these visualizations meaningfully increased trust in AI-assisted diagnoses, demonstrating that model interpretability is as important as accuracy for clinical adoption.
D. Vision Transformers and Advanced Architectures
Chen et al. [6] applied a Vision Transformer (ViT) with self-attention across axial, coronal, and sagittal MRI planes, achieving 97.9% accuracy and outperforming CNN-based models on three-plane inputs. While Vision Transformers show great promise, they typically require substantially larger datasets and computational resources than CNN-based Transfer Learning approaches, making ResNet50 a more practical choice for the dataset scale of this project.
E. Research Gap
The reviewed literature reveals three consistent limitations. First, most systems address binary AD classification rather than the clinically more meaningful four-class staging across Non-Demented, Very Mild Demented, Mild Demented, and Moderate Demented. Second, multi-class classifiers rarely include the preprocessing rigor—resizing, normalization, augmentation—needed to generalize across MRI scanners and protocols. Third, very few systems are deployed as accessible web applications with interpretable outputs such as confidence scores. The proposed ResNet50-based system directly addresses all three gaps.

III. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY
The proposed system follows a seven-stage pipeline: Brain MRI Dataset → Preprocessing → Data Augmentation → ResNet50 Transfer Learning → Feature Extraction → Multi-class Classification → Alzheimer’s Stage Prediction with Confidence Score, with the trained model integrated into a web application for accessible deployment.
A. Brain MRI Dataset
The system is trained and evaluated on the Augmented Alzheimer MRI Dataset, sourced from the open-access Kaggle repository. The dataset comprises Brain MRI images classified into four categories corresponding to Alzheimer’s Disease stages: Non-Demented, Very Mild Demented, Mild Demented, and Moderate Demented. The dataset is partitioned using an 80/20 train-test split with a fixed random seed to ensure reproducible and fair experimental comparison. Exact image counts per class are presented in the Results section.
B. MRI Preprocessing
Raw MRI images undergo a structured preprocessing pipeline before being fed to the model. Each image is resized to 224×224 pixels to match the input specification required by the ResNet50 architecture. Pixel values are normalized to the range [0, 1] by dividing by 255, ensuring consistent scale across images and accelerating model convergence. Where applicable, noise reduction techniques are applied to remove imaging artifacts that may interfere with feature extraction.
C. Data Augmentation
To improve model generalization and reduce the risk of overfitting—particularly for classes with fewer samples such as Moderate Demented—on-the-fly data augmentation is applied during training. Augmentation techniques include horizontal flipping, random rotation within a bounded range, zoom variation, and brightness adjustment. These transformations simulate real-world variability in MRI acquisition and patient positioning, expanding the effective training set without additional data collection.
D. ResNet50 Architecture and Transfer Learning
ResNet50 [7] is a 50-layer deep Convolutional Neural Network that introduced residual (skip) connections to the deep learning literature. These connections allow the gradient signal to bypass one or more convolutional layers via a shortcut path, directly connecting the input of a residual block to its output. This design effectively mitigates the vanishing gradient problem that prevents very deep networks from training reliably, enabling ResNet50 to learn richer and more discriminative feature representations than shallower CNNs.
For this project, ResNet50 is initialized with weights pretrained on the ImageNet dataset—a large-scale benchmark containing over 1 million images across 1,000 categories. Transfer Learning is applied by: (i) loading the pretrained ResNet50 base without its original classification head; (ii) initially freezing all base layers to preserve learned low-level and mid-level features; (iii) adding a Global Average Pooling layer followed by a fully connected Dense layer with four output nodes and Softmax activation, one node per Alzheimer’s stage; and (iv) progressively unfreezing deeper base layers during fine-tuning to adapt higher-level features to the MRI domain. This approach allows the model to leverage rich visual representations learned from ImageNet while specializing for Alzheimer’s stage discrimination from Brain MRI.
E. Feature Extraction and Multi-class Classification
The convolutional and residual blocks of ResNet50 function as a feature extractor, transforming each 224×224 MRI image into a high-dimensional feature vector that encodes spatial, textural, and structural brain characteristics relevant to Alzheimer’s pathology. The Global Average Pooling layer reduces spatial dimensions while retaining the most important channel-wise activations. The final Dense layer with Softmax activation maps the extracted features to posterior probabilities over the four Alzheimer’s stages, producing both a predicted class label and an associated confidence score for each input image.
F. Alzheimer’s Stage Prediction and Confidence Score
For each input Brain MRI image, the system outputs: (1) the predicted Alzheimer’s stage—one of Non-Demented, Very Mild Demented, Mild Demented, or Moderate Demented; and (2) a confidence score expressed as the Softmax probability of the predicted class, providing users with a quantitative measure of the model’s certainty. Both outputs are displayed in the web application interface and are available for further review.
IV. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
The system is built around a modular pipeline architecture connecting a web-based user interface, a backend inference engine, and the trained ResNet50 model. Users upload Brain MRI images through the web application frontend. The backend receives the image, executes the preprocessing pipeline (resize, normalize), performs inference using the trained ResNet50 model, and returns the predicted Alzheimer’s stage and confidence score to the frontend for display.
The ResNet50 model is served as an independent inference module, allowing it to be retrained or replaced without disrupting other system components. The architecture is designed to be modular, enabling future extension to additional disease classification modules.

C. Training Strategy
Training proceeds in two phases. In Phase 1, the ResNet50 base layers are frozen and only the newly added classification head is trained for an initial number of epochs. This allows the head to learn meaningful mappings from ResNet50 features to Alzheimer’s stage labels without disrupting pretrained representations. In Phase 2, selected deeper base layers are unfrozen and the full model is fine-tuned end-to-end with a lower learning rate, allowing the feature extractor to specialize for Brain MRI characteristics while retaining the generalizable representations acquired during ImageNet pretraining.
D. Evaluation Protocol
Model performance is evaluated on the held-out 20% test split using four standard metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score, computed per class and as a weighted overall average from the standard confusion matrix. The same random seed used during dataset splitting is preserved across all training and evaluation runs to ensure reproducibility.

D. Discussion
ResNet50’s residual connections enable the model to extract deep hierarchical features from Brain MRI images without suffering gradient vanishing across its 50 layers. Transfer Learning from ImageNet provides a strong initialization of low-level feature detectors (edges, textures, shapes) that are repurposed for MRI analysis during fine-tuning. The key challenge in four-class Alzheimer’s staging is the visual similarity between adjacent stages—particularly Non-Demented vs. Very Mild Demented—which drives class confusion in the confusion matrix and reduces the weighted F1-score below what would be expected for binary classification tasks.
The confidence score output by the Softmax layer provides an additional dimension of interpretability: predictions with high confidence (e.g., >90%) can be treated with greater reliability than low-confidence predictions, which may benefit from additional clinical review. This mechanism partially compensates for the absence of a full explainability module such as Grad-CAM, which remains a direction for future work.
It is important to note that this system is an AI-assisted research and screening prototype. All predictions should be interpreted by qualified medical professionals, and the system does not replace clinical diagnosis, neurological assessment, or radiological review.
E. Comparison with Existing Works

The primary contribution of the proposed system is not superior accuracy over binary classifiers but the combination of: (a) four-class staging using ResNet50 Transfer Learning; (b) a complete preprocessing and augmentation pipeline; and (c) practical deployment as a web-based prediction system with confidence scores.
VII. FUTURE SCOPE
A. Advanced Architectures
Future iterations could explore EfficientNet, DenseNet, Vision Transformers (ViT), and ConvNeXt as alternative or ensemble backbones. Multi-modal approaches combining Brain MRI features with structured clinical data (MMSE scores, CSF biomarkers, genetic risk factors) have consistently outperformed imaging-only models in the literature and represent a natural extension of this work.
B. Explainability (Grad-CAM)
Integrating Grad-CAM or similar Class Activation Mapping techniques would produce visual heatmaps highlighting the brain regions—hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, parietal lobes—driving each prediction. This would substantially increase clinician trust and enable human-AI collaborative diagnosis rather than black-box decision-making.
C. Clinical Validation
The current system is trained and evaluated on a publicly available Kaggle dataset. Before any clinical application, the model must be validated on large-scale, demographically diverse, hospital-collected Brain MRI datasets to assess real-world generalization across different MRI scanners, acquisition protocols, and patient populations. Regular retraining on updated data is essential to maintain performance.
D. Federated Learning
Training on data from multiple hospitals using federated learning—without raw patient images leaving local institutions—would provide access to larger and more representative training sets while preserving patient privacy and satisfying healthcare data governance requirements such as HIPAA and GDPR.
E. Mobile and Enhanced Web Application
Developing mobile applications for iOS and Android would extend the system’s reach to community health workers and remote clinics. Enhancing the web application with prediction history, longitudinal tracking of a patient’s Alzheimer’s stage over time, and downloadable clinical reports would increase its utility in a real healthcare workflow.
VIII. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a deep learning-based system for automated multi-class Alzheimer’s Disease detection from Brain MRI images using ResNet50 with Transfer Learning. The proposed pipeline—MRI preprocessing, data augmentation, ResNet50 feature extraction, and Softmax multi-class classification—targets four clinically meaningful Alzheimer’s stages: Non-Demented, Very Mild Demented, Mild Demented, and Moderate Demented. ResNet50’s residual connections enable effective gradient propagation through its 50 layers, and Transfer Learning from ImageNet provides a robust feature initialization that is fine-tuned for Brain MRI analysis. The trained model is deployed as a web-based prediction system that returns both a stage prediction and a confidence score, making the system accessible beyond the research laboratory.
The proposed system is presented as an AI-assisted research and screening prototype. It is not intended to replace professional neurological or radiological diagnosis. All outputs should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical assessment by qualified medical professionals. Future work will focus on integrating Grad-CAM explainability, validating on hospital-collected MRI datasets, and exploring advanced architectures and multi-modal fusion to further improve four-class Alzheimer’s staging performance.
